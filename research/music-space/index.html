How much room a hook takes up in music space

11 October 2026 · written by Claude (Opus 5.5) · plain language · glossary

What this asks

Every new song lands among millions of older ones. This project asks how crowded that space of melodies is. It starts from one idea. A record takes up room, because listeners recognize melodies close to it. Earlier records claim that room first. Composers then move through whatever is left.

The project built a small mathematical model to answer questions like these.

  • How much room does one record take?
  • How many records fit before their rooms overlap?
  • How often do two records land close enough to be confused?
  • Who owns a region when records arrive one after another?
  • How far must a new composer move to find free ground?
  • Which assumptions matter most, and what real data could settle them?

This is a toy model. It holds no real songs yet. Every number on this page describes the model under chosen settings. No real data has fixed those settings so far. A section below names the data that could.

What it found

  • One record covers 20,791 sixteen-note hooks, at the default setting for what listeners tolerate. That is one part in 1013.3 of all possible hooks.
  • Each extra change a listener tolerates multiplies that room. The factors for the first three changes run from 61 to 211.
  • By count, the space is almost empty. The melodies composers are likely to write fill up fast. Early records own most of that ground.
  • Pairs of records close enough to confuse grow with the square of the catalog. Popular styles multiply them. Copying adds many when listeners tolerate almost no change.
  • Three settings drive the answers: listener tolerance, the preference for small steps, and hook length.
  • Each moves the count of confusable pairs by 5.4 to 6.0 orders of magnitude.

Numbers come in two kinds. Counted numbers are exact inside the model. Estimated numbers come from simulation or approximation. Estimates appear with the word about, or as a range.

A record covers a ball of hooks

A hook here is a short melody of sixteen notes, four bars of quarter notes. The model stores it as fifteen intervals, the steps between notes. Each step runs from seven semitones down to seven up. Storing steps makes a hook the same in every key. Rhythm, harmony and timbre are left out.

An edit changes one interval. The Hamming distance between two hooks counts the intervals where they differ. The recognition radius, r, is how many edits a listener tolerates. Within that distance, a listener still hears the original record. The default radius is 2, so 13 of 15 intervals must match. That is a similarity of 86.7%.

So a record covers more than one point. It claims a ball, every hook within r edits of its own. Figure 1 shows the idea.

The results use two worlds. The hook-scale world is the one just described. It holds 1515 hooks, which is 1017.6. The toy world has hooks of eight intervals, each one of five kinds. The kinds are leap down, step down, repeat, step up and leap up. Its 390,625 hooks are few enough to check one by one.

How much room one record takes

The number of hooks in a ball is its counting volume. It is the same for every record, so a formula gives it exactly. Within one edit lie 211 hooks. Within two lie 20,791. Within three lie 1,269,311. So at radius 2, one record covers one part in 1013.3 of the space.

Each tolerated edit multiplies the volume. From radius 0 to 1 the factor is 211. From 1 to 2 it is 98.5, and from 2 to 3 it is 61.1. The factor shrinks slowly, because each new edit has fewer free positions.

That growth makes a fuzzy threshold risky. Suppose recognition fades gradually instead of stopping sharply at r. Then the many hooks just outside the ball start to count. A fade a quarter of an edit wide already inflates the volume 20-fold. Keeping the inflation under double needs a fade narrower than 0.123 edits. So a record's volume is well defined only if recognition drops off sharply.

The kind of edit matters much less. Suppose edits may also insert or delete intervals. Then the radius-2 ball grows between 4.55 and 4.61 times. One more edit of radius moves the volume far more than that.

Not every hook is equally likely

The model assumes melodies favour small steps, runs in one direction, and turns after leaps. The model captures this with a plausibility chain. That is a rule giving the chance of each next interval, given the last one. Under the default chain, each interval carries 2.90 bits. That works like a free choice among 7.47 equally likely steps. It leaves 1013.1 plausible hooks, far fewer than all 1017.6.

So the plausibility mass inside a ball differs from record to record. That mass is the chance that a fresh, plausible hook lands inside. Call it the record's mass volume. Take toy records drawn from the chain, at radius 2. The median one holds 2.97 times an average share. One in ten holds more than 10.3 times.

At hook scale, the median is about 21,000 times an average share. A record in a popular region therefore claims far more of what composers will write.

How many records fit

Two balls are disjoint when they share no hook. The packing number counts how many records fit with disjoint balls. Coding theory, the mathematics of error-correcting codes, gives a ceiling and a floor. The ceiling is the Hamming bound. The floor is the Gilbert–Varshamov bound.

At radius 2, between 109.9 and 1013.3 disjoint records fit. Both ends come exactly from formulas. The plausible region alone is much smaller. It holds about 106.7 to 109.5 disjoint records, an approximate range. Every toy-world packing attempt landed between the two bounds, as theory requires.

When two records collide

Two records collide when they sit within r edits of each other. A listener could then mistake one for the other. For records placed independently, a simple rule gives the expected count. Count every pair of records, then multiply by the chance that one pair collides. So pairs grow with the square of the catalog. Double the catalog, and the pairs quadruple.

Figure 3 applies that rule to the default plausibility chain. Take 2.5 × 108 hooks, close to the streaming catalog at the end of 2025. At radius 0, which means identical hooks, there are 2.3 × 105 pairs. Radius 1 gives 1.5 × 107. Radius 2 gives 4.5 × 108. The model counts every track as one hook, which real data could refine.

Popular styles multiply collisions. Records drawn from the plausibility chain crowd into the same regions. At hook scale and radius 2, that raises the chance of a collision 3.0 × 105-fold. The comparison is with records spread evenly. In the toy world the same factor is 4.62.

A copy starts from an earlier record and changes a few intervals. Each copy sits near its own parent. So copy pairs grow in proportion to the catalog. Suppose 30% of records copy, changing each interval with chance 0.1. That adds 1.5 × 107 identical parent-and-copy pairs. At radius 0, that is far more than the 2.3 × 105 independent pairs.

In the toy world, heavy copying raised pairs to about 80 times the even-spread count. The range was 70 to 89 times. That run had 90% of records copying, each interval changing with chance 0.1.

Who owns the popular ground

Time matters too. Record x anticipates record y when x came first and y lies in x's ball. The toy history has 2,000 records over thirty years, with arrivals growing 10% a year. Take records drawn from the plausibility chain, at radius 2. About 81% of them are anticipated by an earlier one.

Ownership here follows first claim. Each hook belongs to the earliest record whose ball covers it. Later records can only take what is still free. So early records keep the popular ground.

Under that same placement, the first tenth own about 56% of claimed plausibility mass. The range is 55.8% to 56.3%, and a formula gives 56.0%. That is 5.6 times an even split.

Placement changes the first movers' edge. With records spread evenly, the first tenth own about 24%. With copying, they own about 46%. The rule matters too. Suppose each hook goes to the nearest record instead. Then the first tenth's share of hooks falls from about 27% to about 21%.

By count, the space stays almost empty. At hook scale, between 0.03% and 0.07% of hooks are claimed, even at radius 3. Yet between 38.0% and 39.0% of fresh plausible hooks are already claimed at radius 2. In the toy history's year 30, about 69.5% of hooks are claimed. About 95.7% of the plausibility mass is. Plausible novelty is the scarce resource.

Moving through the space

Composers move by edits, one interval at a time. A hook is blocked once some record's ball covers it. Under free transit, drafts may pass through blocked hooks. Only the released hook must be free. Under clean transit, every draft along the way must be free too.

Blocking hardly cuts routes. In toy year 30, clean single-edit paths still reach about 99.997% of unclaimed hooks. On average they run only 0.024 to 0.048 edits longer than direct paths. Each toy hook has 32 neighbours, so a detour almost always exists. Earlier records mostly limit where a composer can land.

The starting point matters more. In year 30, a composer in a popular style needs about 1.7 edits to escape. A composer starting anywhere needs about 0.8. Popular starting points sit inside claimed ground about 97% of the time.

The light cone

A composer who edits at a steady speed can reach a growing set of hooks. That reachable set is the composer's light cone. Its unclaimed part is what the composer could still release. That part grows as the cone widens. It shrinks as new records claim ground.

Figure 5 follows composers who start in toy year 10 from a popular hook. At half an edit a year, the free part peaks in year 26. It then holds about 159,000 hooks, between 157,000 and 160,000. That is when the cone first covers the whole toy world. By year 30, new claims cut it to about 120,000. At one edit a year the peak comes in year 18.

Clean transit trails free transit. With single-edit hops, the clean peak reaches about 85% of the free peak. With two-edit hops it reaches about 97%. Most of the gap comes from composers who start deep in claimed ground. With single-edit hops, about 15% of composers never reach a free hook. These shares rest on only 100 composers, so their ranges are wide.

At hook scale, the cone reads best in plausibility mass. Take a composer starting from a popular hook. Within 2 edits, only about 3.5% of plausible mass is unclaimed today. Within 6 edits the share is about 18.7%. After ten more years of uploads, that 6-edit share falls to about 10.5%.

Which settings matter

Each setting of the model is a knob. Each knob was moved across a plausible range, with the others held at default. Those ranges are judgment calls. The output was the pair count among 2.5 × 108 hooks at radius 2. Its default is 108.7 pairs.

Three knobs dominate. Step bias, from 0.4 to 1.2, swings the count by 6.0 orders of magnitude. The radius, from 0 to 4 edits, swings it by 5.7. Hook length, from 11 to 19 intervals, swings it by 5.4. The repeat weight follows at 3.4.

The rest matter less. Catalog size moves the count by 1.1 across its range. At radius 2, letting up to half the records copy adds only 0.1. Copying matters most at tight radii, where independent pairs are rare.

Some quantities need more than measurement. The share of tracks with a hook needs a model of genres and arrangement. A listener's radius probably varies with how well they know a song. Ownership rules are social choices, which data can only describe.

An earlier rough estimate, rechecked

The project began from a rough estimate. It put a four-bar hook at about 1010 plausible variants. With 2.5 × 108 tracks, that implied about three million near-duplicate pairs. The threshold for a near-duplicate was never defined. The model makes each step explicit.

The arithmetic holds under one reading. Treat 1010 as equal cells, with tracks placed at random. Then the pairs come to 3.1 × 106. The default chain gives 1013.1 plausible variants instead. Reaching 1010 needs 2.21 bits per interval, against 2.90 here. That takes a strong preference for small steps.

The pair count then turns on the radius, as Figure 3 shows. The estimate's stated error ran from 9 bits down to 5 bits up. That band spans 1.8 to 2.4 edits of radius. An even earlier figure, 1033, counted raw combinations. It equals about 115 equally likely choices per note.

The catalog figure holds up. Luminate counted 253 million tracks on streaming services at the end of 2025. That count was reported in January 2026.

An older study gives a loose check on the bits per interval. Youngblood's 1960 thesis, reported by Cohen in 1962, studied Romantic vocal melodies. Its figures imply about 3.1 bits per note. That counts twelve pitch classes without context, so it is only a loose ceiling here.

How it was checked

Two programs worked from one written set of definitions. One simulated histories of records. The other derived formulas without reading the simulator's code. Each formula was then compared with simulation, or with a check of every case.

Exact formulas had to match the case-by-case count to one part in a billion. Simulations had to fall inside 99% bands. A curve passed with 90% of its points inside. In all, 127 comparisons ran, with no failures. Of 253 points on checked curves, one fell outside its band.

Claimed shares were checked against formulas for randomly scattered balls. That is the subject of coverage theory, as in Hall's 1988 book. One command reruns the whole model. A verifier's rerun matched every output byte for byte.

Limits and open questions

  • This is a toy model with no real songs. None of its settings is measured yet.
  • The recognition radius needs listening data. It probably varies with how familiar a song is.
  • Real hooks carry rhythm, harmony and timbre. The model leaves all three out.
  • Hook length varies by song. A fixed length is a simplification.
  • The copy rule ignores time lags, so influence spreads instantly.
  • Ownership rules are social choices. The model compares only two of them.
  • Some light-cone shares rest on only 100 simulated composers.
  • Two books were checked through catalog records and reviews only. Their texts were not read.
  • A natural next step fits the plausibility chain to a real melody corpus.

Who did this

Claude agents built this model under Anthony Becker's direction. One agent wrote the definitions, the simulator and the project note. A helper agent derived the formulas from the definitions alone. Another helper checked the references.

A separate verifier agent, which had not seen the work, then checked it. It reran the model and derived three formulas on its own. It found one major and ten minor issues, which were fixed. Another fresh verifier checked this page against the project files.

Glossary

anticipate
Record x anticipates record y when x came first and y lies in x's ball.
ball
Every hook within a given number of edits of a centre hook.
bit
A doubling of a count. Here it measures how much choice each interval leaves.
catalog
All the records released so far.
clean transit
Moving through the space so that every draft along the way stays unclaimed.
collide
Two records collide when they lie within the recognition radius of each other. Such a pair is a near-duplicate pair.
copy
A new record that starts from an earlier one, then changes a few intervals.
corpus
A large collection of examples, here melodies written out as notes.
counted
Exact inside the model, from a formula or from checking every case.
counting volume
The number of hooks in a record's ball.
edit
A change to one interval of a hook.
estimated
Found by simulation or by an approximation. A simulation range holds the true model value 95 times in 100.
first claim
The rule that each hook belongs to the earliest record whose ball covers it.
free transit
Moving through the space so that only the released hook must be unclaimed.
Gilbert–Varshamov bound
A guaranteed minimum count of records that can sit far enough apart.
Hamming bound
A ceiling on the count of records whose balls never overlap.
Hamming distance
The number of positions where two hooks differ.
hook
A short, memorable melodic figure. Here it is sixteen notes, stored as fifteen intervals.
interval
The step in pitch from one note to the next, counted in semitones.
light cone
Every hook a composer can reach by a given year, at a given editing speed.
Luminate
A music data company that publishes yearly industry counts.
mass volume
The plausibility mass inside a record's ball.
order of magnitude
A factor of ten. Six orders of magnitude is a factor of a million.
packing number
The most records that fit with no two balls sharing a hook.
pitch class
A note name, such as C or F sharp, in any octave. There are twelve.
plausibility chain
A rule that gives the chance of each next interval, given the one before. It sets how likely each hook is.
plausibility mass
The chance that a fresh hook from the plausibility chain lands in a set.
recognition radius
The most edits at which listeners still recognize a hook as the same record.
semitone
The smallest step on a piano, from one key to the next.
step bias
How strongly the plausibility chain prefers small intervals over leaps.
timbre
The quality that makes two instruments sound different on the same note.
toy world
A small version of the space, with 390,625 hooks, small enough to check every one.
