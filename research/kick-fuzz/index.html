Anthony Becker / Research / Kick fuzz
Research · 13 November 2026 · written by Claude (Opus 5.5)

Fuzz, not a click

HW002 is a hard techno Short made with zpkt. Next to its references the kick was the problem. They all have a nasty mid-high fuzz, and ours didn't. Matching the spectrum put back the energy but not the fuzz. Measuring the texture of the mid-highs did better: how long they last, how dense they are, how compressed they are. So did finding the device in HW002's chain that had been turning fuzz into a click.

The spectrum said what, not how

The first comparison was spectral. A reference's kick can't be soloed, but in a drop the kick lands on every beat. So every beat of the drop was lined up on its kick onset, and the median spectrogram over all 64 beats was taken. That keeps what repeats on every beat and drops what doesn't: stabs, vocals, risers. The slice from the onset to the next sixteenth is the kick. HW002's mix went through the same method, and so did HW002's kick on its own. Below 4 kHz the two agreed to within 1 dB, so the isolation was clean there. Against the four references, our kick was 8–9 dB short at 2.5–4 kHz and had 2–5 dB too much body at 80–200 Hz.

The obvious fixes followed. HW002's kick rack already had a fuzz layer: a second kick sample, 052, high-passed and run into a COLDFIRE distortion. Turning it up, unhiding a 3.4 kHz cut and fitting an EQ to the gap put the spectrum inside the references' range. Anthony's verdict:

All of the options add some high freq info but it's basically a soft noise, like an echo of a stick hit. The reference is fuzzy and compressed.Anthony, on the A/B page. He named KSMS's Roses Of Flesh And Blood the canonical reference.

Measuring texture

Band levels can't tell a stick hit from fuzz. Both put energy at 3 kHz. What separates them is how that energy behaves, so each kick's mid-highs (1–6 kHz) were measured over its median beat:

  • Hold. How long the mid-highs stay within 12 dB of their peak. A tick is gone in a few milliseconds. Fuzz made by clipping the kick lasts as long as what it clips.
  • Tail. How much is left 100–170 ms after the hit, before an offbeat would land.
  • Density. Spectral flatness of the band, from 0 for a few clean harmonics to 1 for noise. Fuzz is dense.
  • Crest. Peak against RMS of the band's envelope. Compressed is low.
  • Level. The band's share of the kick's energy.

Combined into one distance from KSMS, the other three references land 1.7–2.9 away. HW002's kick as it was is 6.2 away. Its mid-highs held for 8 ms with a flatness of 0.11. That's the stick hit, in numbers.

Where the click came from

The 052 layer was high-passed at 958 Hz before COLDFIRE. Nothing low enough to clip reached the distortion, only the click. COLDFIRE had been turning a click into a louder click. Every option that turned that layer up added more stick hit.

Fuzz comes from clipping something with a body. Ten rounds of options went through HW002's own kick chain in Live, each bounced as the arranger's kick loop and measured against KSMS:

  • Roar on the whole kick group clipped the rumble too. The fuzz never stopped: it held for over 300 ms.
  • Roar on the kick track clipped the kick's long low boom, with the same result.
  • Noise or bit-crushed grain on top didn't make the band denser.
  • The measure itself had a bug for a while. The onset finder locked onto the rumble's swell between kicks and smeared our median beats by ±30 ms. Our loops are bounced on the grid, so they're now read on it.
  • What worked used Roar's multiband mode, already in HW002's kick group and switched off. Only the kick's low band (under 200 Hz) went into a Tri Fold shaper at 24 dB drive, with a high-pass after the shaper keeping only what the folding adds, 35% under the clean kick. This body fuzz scored closest to KSMS, at 2.2.

The ear picked the punch

Anthony picked a different option: punch fuzz. It folds only the middle band, 200 Hz–2 kHz. That's the kick's punch, which decays faster than its sub and where the rumble has almost nothing. Its mid-highs are the densest of anything made (flatness 0.40, KSMS 0.32), and its tail sits in the references' range (−20.7 dB; theirs −16.5 to −20.4). It holds for a shorter time than the body fuzz, and it scored a little further from KSMS (2.8). The whole-spectrum match came second: an EQ Eight fitted to KSMS's isolated kick, with the loudness kept where it was. Matched, it scores 2.3.

The numbers ranked the body fuzz first and the ear chose the punch. Density and tail are where the punch fuzz is closest to KSMS. Hold is where the body fuzz is. So the ear seems to weigh grain and decay over sustain, and the distance should probably weight them that way too.

How it was heard

Every kick here was rendered in Ableton Live from HW002's own set. Each was turned so that it played at the same loudness as before: a fuzzier kick isn't allowed to win by being louder. Anthony compared them on two pages on his phone. One is a kick-only A/B/C page, with each kick's spectrum pre-computed frame by frame and drawn as it plays. The other is a page of full mixes, with the kick swapped in. The pages only play what Live rendered; zpkt doesn't let the browser make or change sound. There's no audio here: the references are commercial releases, and HW002's kick is built from purchased samples.

Tools, in zpkt's HW002 arranger: kickiso.py isolates kicks by the beat, kicktexture.py measures their texture, kickfuzz.py makes options from HW002's chain in Live and renders them, and kickdesign.py pre-computes the kick page's spectra.
