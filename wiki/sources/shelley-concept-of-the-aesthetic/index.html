What it is

James Shelley wrote this entry for the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. It surveys what 'aesthetic' means across five uses: object, judgment, attitude, experience and value.

What it says

  • The modern concept descends from 18th-century taste, built on two theses: immediacy and disinterest.
  • Kant turned disinterest into a contrast between moral and aesthetic judgment.
  • The entry says Danto's case ended the heyday of artistic formalism.
  • Dickie argues that a different purpose need not mean different attention. The entry accepts this point.
  • Dickie says Beardsley confuses features of objects with features of experience.
  • Hedonism is the prevailing answer on value, but recent critics dispute it.
  • New theories either reduce aesthetic value to another value or treat it as basic.

What evidence it gives

The entry argues from close readings of texts by Hume, Kant, Sibley, Dickie and Beardsley. It uses thought experiments, such as Danto's look-alike objects. It also uses a historical case of Athenians fining a playwright. It offers no empirical data.

Interests and limits

The entry is one philosopher's critical survey, not a neutral summary. It judges some positions as weaker. It disputes Danto's step from the limits of form to the limits of the aesthetic. Shelley is listed among authors sympathetic to the Socratic solution. It covers Anglophone debate and gives other traditions little space. The main text was read in full but the bibliography was skipped.
