What it is

John McCarthy, then at MIT, describes a recursive way to define functions. McCarthy also describes a programming system built on it. The paper covers the notation, the symbolic data, a universal evaluator and the machine implementation. It ends with a linear variant and a link between flowcharts and recursion.

What it says

  • McCarthy says LISP was built on the IBM 704. It served experiments with the Advice Taker.
  • McCarthy presents conditional expressions as believed new. They allow a convenient recursive way to define functions.
  • The paper says five elementary functions, with conditionals and recursion, can express all computable functions.
  • The paper describes a universal function that is both a theoretical model and an interpreter.
  • Programmers need not return freed storage. Reclamation runs automatically when free storage runs out.
  • As of February 1960, compiled functions ran about 60 times as fast as interpreted ones.
  • It says lambda notation cannot name recursive functions, so the paper adds a label notation.

What evidence it gives

The paper gives formal definitions and worked examples. These include substitution, differentiation and a step-by-step trace of the evaluator. It describes the working IBM 704 system. Its performance figures are the compiled speed ratio and a reclamation time of several seconds. Neither comes with a benchmark.

Interests and limits

The author was in the group that built the system. The paper is the designer's own account. It is Part I only. The prose was read in full. The formulas and figures were images and were not read.
