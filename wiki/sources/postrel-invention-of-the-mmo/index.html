What it is

Virginia Postrel is a contributing editor at Works in Progress. The essay recounts how Habitat arose, how it worked and what happened in play. It starts with the Commodore 64 and the Q-Link network. It then follows the game from its pilot to its public launch as Club Caribe.

What it says

  • Postrel quotes Alex Handy calling Habitat the first virtual world.
  • Postrel says Habitat ran on 64 kilobytes of memory and 300-baud modems.
  • Postrel says Q-Link charged $3 an hour in 1986, while CompuServe charged $8 off-peak.
  • Postrel says a pricing error let players quintuple Habitat's token supply overnight.
  • Postrel says one player solved a weeks-long treasure hunt in eight hours.
  • Postrel says the public launch was renamed Club Caribe, dropping weapons and some fantasy elements.
  • Postrel says players spontaneously formed performing arts groups despite slow connections.

What evidence it gives

The essay relies on interviews with Alex Handy, Chip Morningstar, F Randall Farmer, Stuart Cass. It quotes the 1990 Morningstar and Farmer paper, Compute! magazine and Jesper Juul's book. It gives prices, memory sizes and one money-supply incident. It presents no tests or measurements of its own.

Interests and limits

Most accounts are recollections from the creators and fans, decades later. The essay does not state any financial interest. The subtitle says 1982 for the first massively multiplayer world. The text gives a public launch in 1989. The essay covers one game and does not compare it with other early online worlds.
