A generic non-invasive neuromotor interface for human-computer interaction

A Nature paper describing a wristband that reads muscle signals to control computers.

Author

Subject
Science and medicine

What it is

CTRL-labs at Reality Labs wrote this open-access paper. The first two authors are Patrick Kaifosh and Thomas Reardon. It sets out to build a computer input that needs no calibration. The device reads electrical signals from muscles at the wrist. Tasks were cursor control, gesture input and handwriting.

What it says

  • The paper describes a wristband with 16 bipolar channels that records muscle signals.
  • It says models trained on thousands of people work for new users without calibration.
  • Median closed-loop speeds were 0.66 and 0.88 acquisitions per second, and 20.9 words per minute.
  • Models trained on one person did poorly when tested on another person.
  • Error fell as training participants grew, following a power law.
  • The sEMG decoders were slower than a trackpad, a game controller and open-loop handwriting.
  • Offline, personalizing a handwriting model gave a 16% median gain from 20 minutes of data.

What evidence it gives

Training data came from 162 to 6,627 participants, depending on the task. Closed-loop tests used 17, 24 and 20 new participants. Baselines were a trackpad, a Joy-Con controller and open-loop handwriting. Code and recordings from 300 participants are released.

Interests and limits

The authors are from CTRL-labs at Reality Labs. Data were collected at Meta facilities or vendor sites. The decoders were slower than every baseline tested. Handwriting needed coaching for about 80% of test participants. Training used able-bodied participants, and the paper says clinical use is unclear. Figures and videos were not viewed.
