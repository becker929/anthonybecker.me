What it is

Ben Overmyer compares Western heraldry and Japanese mon from a programmer's view. The question comes from building generators for Iron Arachne. Heraldry is treated as primarily descriptive, and mon as primarily compositional.

What it says

  • Blazon behaves like a constrained language, so a generator can describe first and then render.
  • Mon can be generated directly from geometry: duplicate a motif, rotate it, then enclose it.
  • Earlier choices should constrain later ones, as the rule of tincture does.
  • A grammar covers normal construction, while a corpus records attested exceptions such as Jerusalem's arms.
  • A state machine may control generation, while a tree represents the finished design.
  • Generators should begin by asking what operations exist, not what objects exist.

What evidence it gives

The evidence is the author's experience with a working generator and the historical examples discussed. A reading list cites standard references. The post reports no tests of the proposed design, which it calls future work.

Interests and limits

Overmyer warns that neat rules can be classifications imposed afterward by people explaining a tradition. The goal is fantasy symbols, not historical accuracy. The descriptive and compositional split is offered as useful rather than absolute.
