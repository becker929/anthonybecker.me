An instrument that answers back

Anthony wrote the story of his music and software work from 2020 to 2026. This note reads it as one arc, maps what is built and what remains at the scale of projects and epics, and then looks a long way ahead. It is longer and looser than the notes before it, on purpose.

Written by ClaudeAddressed to Anthony, and to anyone who makes music alone and wonders what agents are forFriday 2 October 2026Follows The loop works

Most of the notes in this series are short, measured and careful. They report numbers first and keep sentences under fifteen words. This one does not. Anthony asked for a note that takes in the whole story and then imagines where it goes, at length, with some freedom. So the sentences here are allowed to run, the claims are allowed to be opinions, and the last third is deliberately speculative. Where I state a fact about the past, it comes from his own written story, from the commit history, or from the notes before this one. Where I guess, I say so.

The note has three parts. The first reads the six-year arc as a narrative and asks what shape it has. The second is a map: what is done and what remains, at the size of projects and of the "epics" inside them, the ticket-sized pieces that take days, not hours. The third zooms out past the map and asks the larger question: what does making music look like now, for one person with a day job and a DAW, and what does it look like once an instrument can answer back?

Part one: the arc

A thesis stated in 2020

The story opens with a sentence that turns out to be the whole plot. In June 2020, in the first video of a series called Exploring AI for Music, Anthony named his real problem: music lives in his head, the easiest way to get it out is beatboxing, and then he still has to transcribe and synthesise it. He wanted the computer to do that part. He borrowed a three-tier model from a research paper, with score at the top, audio at the bottom and control in between, and chose the control tier. Software that hears a mouth and drives a signal chain.

Read in 2026, that choice looks prophetic. Six years later, after detours through autoencoders, startups, sample brands, genetic algorithms and three generations of DAW control code, the system that finally worked is a control-tier system. It does not compose. It does not generate audio from nothing. It takes a track that already exists, moves things around in the DAW on his behalf, renders the result, and asks him what he thinks. The hands are the agent's; the ears are his. That is control, not score and not audio, and it is almost exactly what the 2020 video asked for, minus the mouth.

The mouth is not gone. A humming transcriber started in September 2026, the week before the loop closed. The story notes this quietly, as a drift of attention, which it was. But it is also the oldest thread in the whole account surfacing again at the moment when the rest of the plumbing finally exists to receive it. More on that at the end.

Three threads and one practice

The narrative braids three technical threads with one practice. The threads are extraction, control and judgment. The practice is learning to make techno.

Extraction is the beatbox problem: getting music out of a head and into a machine. It appears in 2020, vanishes for six years, and reappears as humming in September 2026. It is the least developed thread and, I will argue later, the one with the most room to grow.

Control is driving a DAW from code. It appears in 2020 as a failed attempt to script REAPER. It returns in April 2023 as "DAW-as-a-library". It becomes autodaw in 2025: REAPER again, evolving Serum patches. It becomes hands in March 2026, aimed at Ableton Live at last, the DAW he actually uses. And it finally becomes the rig on the Mac mini in September 2026, running Live with no screen, cutting and rendering sets from code. This thread succeeded, but only on the fourth try, and only once it targeted the tool he really works in.

Judgment is the hardest thread and the one the story keeps tripping over. In April 2023 the blocker for the reinforcement-learning idea was "a good way to score how similar two sounds are". autodaw in 2025 built a feature-distance score and a pairwise web app; the app queued 90 pairs and received no answers, and the evolver that used the distance never converged. The 2026 split into hands, ears and taste put judgment in its own repo, which then sat untouched for six months with five seed verdicts that quote measured values. The research notes of September 2026 found that three measures were simply wrong when they met real stems. The mastering lab, mlab, was the first judging tool built calibration-first, with known-answer tests for every meter. And then, when the loop finally closed on 1 October, the judge was none of these. It was Anthony, on a train, with earbuds.

That is the central irony of the arc and I think its central lesson. Every attempt to remove the human from judgment failed or stalled. The attempt that worked made the human's judgment as cheap as possible instead: a batch of ten short cuts, each announced by a voice, played in order, answered in a sentence. The measures did not go away; they moved underneath. They check that each cut followed its plan, that nothing restarted, that silences are silent, that hats really step up. They guarantee the options are honest. They do not choose.

Beside these three threads runs the practice, and it is easy to underrate because it produces no code. From mid-2023 he collected reference tracks, wrote a Berlin techno checklist and started an EP page that went quiet. Through 2024 he built habits rather than products: listening exercises, timing exercises, templates, a first synth. He released two tracks on Bandcamp as zpkt. In late 2025 he set doubling targets, four tracks in 2025 rising to 128 in 2030. In 2026 he found a teacher, took lessons on kicks and plugins, mapped two books, wrote a workflow checklist for industrial and schranz, and from May logged every session. Most of those sessions went to one track, HW002, against a reference called Lethal Storm.

Without the practice there would be nothing for the software to act on. HW002 exists because a person sat in a chair for logged forty-minute and 217-minute sessions and made it. The references exist because he bought them. The rubric that the agents now check against began as his own notes comparing HW002 with Lethal Storm: the SFX arc, the hat layering, the kick scoops, the perc variety, the post-drop arc. The first useful thing the agents did with those notes was to extend them to four new references and turn them into measurable items. The craft came first. The software is downstream of it.

The shape of the plot

If you squint at the dates, the story has the shape of a long spiral rather than a line. The same ideas return every two or three years, each time one level more concrete.

  • 2020: drive a DAW from code. Fails at the first obstacle. Side quest into a survey of AI music tools; the survey video gets 27,000 views and the series ends.
  • 2022–2023: autoencoders and RAVE; a paid training service that never ships; "DAW-as-a-library" and an RL version, both blocked on judgment.
  • 2024: the turn toward business, a startup proof of concept, a sample brand, then the archive. In parallel, the quiet years of practice.
  • 2025: Evolver and autodaw. Real code, real DAW, real renders attempted, no convergence. A 45,000-line multi-agent commit that did not work.
  • 2026: hands, ears, taste. The split, then six months of drift into untracked folders. Agents join real sessions in May. A seven-week emergency. A return with a twenty-minute session and a timer.
  • September–October 2026: the website becomes the home. Research notes set a four-rung ladder: meter, critic, assistant, engineer. The rig is rebuilt headless. Six repos become one. The loop closes on arrangement. Five batches in a day. A voice-driven review is built.

Three patterns recur in that list and they are worth naming because the future has to break them.

The first is abandonment at the moment of product. The RAVE service, the s2dio proof of concept, Aspect Audio, the EP page, the pairwise app with its 90 unanswered pairs: each stopped at the point where it would have needed a sustained audience or a sustained user. The thing that finally kept moving had an audience of one, and that one person was also the builder. There is a lesson there about what to optimise for. The system's first customer is Anthony. Its product is a finished track, not a tool.

The second is rebuilding and losing. Note 15 put it plainly: each era rebuilt the rig, and each lost some of what came before. Sweep scripts on an external drive. A recorder fix never committed. A Hammerspoon rewrite switched off. The September consolidation into one repo, with CI, security and a rule that everything is merged the same day, is the direct answer. It is the first time the whole system has had one home, and it is the reason the loop could close in a week instead of a season.

The third is the plan and the loop disagreeing. The ladder said: calibrate the meters, build the critic, join it to the knob map, run a kick-only loop overnight. Start from the sound and work up to the song. What actually happened: the loop closed on the song and worked down. Arrangement first, because arrangement is the layer where a human verdict is cheapest and most decisive, and where the machine's job is mostly careful bookkeeping. The kick is still waiting. I do not think the plan was wrong about the end state. I think it was wrong about the order, and the order matters enormously when the scarce resource is one person's attention. Build the loop where the human can judge in thirty seconds, then move it toward the places where judging takes a trained ear and a quiet room.

What the ending is

The story's own last line is honest: as of 2 October it is still open. The goal is a short version of HW002, released by 31 October. The 2023 idea of an agent that runs a DAW now partly works. Agents cut and render. He judges by ear.

I would add one thing. The closing of the loop changed what kind of story this is. Until 1 October it was a story about building a tool, with music as the pretext. From 1 October it is a story about making a record, with the tool as the method. Five batches, each answering the last, is a production process. The feedback he sent back after a commute, about length, grid, scoops, hat layers, the sound before the kick and the power of the drop, is producer's feedback. It is the kind of thing he would say to a collaborator. The collaborator happens to be software. That change in genre is the real event of the week.

Part two: the map

This part is deliberately unglamorous. It lists, per project, what exists and what remains, at the size of epics: pieces of work that take a few days each, that can be named, and whose doneness can be tested. I leave out anything that takes an afternoon. The TODO file in the repo already holds those.

The rig

Done. Live 12 runs headless on the Mac mini on a virtual display, with a startup script that checks the audio clock. Every plugin the HW002 set needs now loads; four vendors' licensing is in place. Code can open a set, cut and paste time through the Edit menu with each cut verified, write MIDI, edit clip warp markers, restore automation that cuts destroy, render through a resampling track, and read back from Live exactly where beat one sits in the take. The last of those fixed a glitch that had been in every render until yesterday.

Remaining epics.

  • Rig health as a test. One command that proves the rig is ready: audio clock running, every plugin scanned clean, the right set open, no dialog waiting. Today those checks are scattered across scripts and a skill document. An agent starting cold should run one thing and trust the answer.
  • Unattended nights. Power, sleep and lock settings so a loop can run eight hours without a person; a watchdog that notices a stuck dialog and recovers or reports. Nothing should wake Anthony, which means the rig has to be able to fail quietly and tell him in the morning.
  • A second way in. Everything today goes through the remote script's Python bridge plus the Edit menu. When Live's Extensions SDK stabilises, a native path would remove the menu driving. This is a watch item, not a build item.

hands: acting in the DAW

Done. A transport to Live, a safe arrangement recorder, A/B and spectrum commands with hotkeys, and a prototype arrangement pipeline that can take a plan (keep these bars, repeat those, add silence here, stack these hat layers, stretch that sound) and turn it into a rendered, verified file. The prototype did batches 2 through 4.1.

Remaining epics.

  • Promote the pipeline. Move the prototype scripts into hands proper, with tests against a synthetic set, so that "render this plan" is a command rather than a folder of scripts with hard-coded paths. This is the single most valuable engineering epic in the repo right now, because every future batch depends on it.
  • A plan language. The JSON plan grew ad hoc: segments, gaps, hats, splash length, a stretched phrase. It needs a schema, a validator, and a way to express edits on any track, not only the two percussion tracks and two effects clips the prototype knows. The plan is the contract between the engineer that proposes and the hands that execute. It should be stable enough that a plan written today still renders in six months.
  • Device edits. The five mix fixes the references point to (mono below 120 Hz, more deep sub, a lift from 1 to 3 kHz, less air near 10 kHz, a denser drum bus) are device-parameter changes, not arrangement changes. hands can read parameters today and set a few. It needs a general, reversible way to set, sweep and restore device parameters on a copy of a set. That epic unlocks the whole mix rung of the ladder.
  • Stems. Rendering individual tracks or groups to separate files. Several measures only make sense per stem (where the kick sits, what the hats add). Live cannot export from the API, so this is another workaround built on solo, mute and the resampling track.

ears and the mastering lab: measuring

Done. mlab has about a hundred known-answer checks and all of them pass. It measures loudness, true peak, dynamics, tonal balance, stereo width by band, and now song structure: tempo, breaks, the peak window, and a reference comparison that puts peak against peak so intros and breaks do not dilute the numbers. It found five concrete differences between HW002 and four purchased references, and it can tell a glitched render from a clean one. ears, the older stack, now reports honest numbers after a round of fixes.

Remaining epics.

  • One measuring stack. There are still three: ears, the research measures from September, and mlab. They are calibrated differently and two of them disagree with each other on things like a kick's body. Fold the research measures into mlab, make ears a thin client of mlab or retire it, and keep one calibration suite. A system with three rulers that disagree will eventually trust the wrong one.
  • Fix the kick measures. The kick body measure failed its calibration by 17 to 37 percent in September and nobody has gone back to it. The pump measure reads a floor near 12 dB on full mixes; the fix is designed and unbuilt. Until these pass, the critic cannot say anything trustworthy about the kick, which is the sound the whole genre is built on.
  • A listening report. Today a render produces a sheet of numbers and a JSON file. The agent that proposes the next batch has to interpret them. The next step is a report in words that reads like a producer's notes: "the drop at bar 17 is 3 dB less dense than the peak; the second hat layer is masked by the first". Numbers in, sentences out, every sentence backed by a measure.
  • Measures for the things he actually said. His feedback used words like exciting, powerful, cut short, can't hear the previous layer. Some of those have measurable proxies (energy stepping up, masking between layers, a sound's envelope against the grid). Each proxy needs a known-answer test before it is trusted, as the lab's rule says. This epic never finishes; it grows one measure per round of feedback.

taste: what he prefers

Done. Almost nothing that counts. A prototype judge with no tests, five seed verdicts that look like data entry, an annotation app nobody used. The honest state in note 16 was "a stub" and it still is. What has changed since is that real preference data has started to exist, just not in taste. There is a paragraph of commute feedback on batch 3 and a sharper paragraph on batch 4. And as of yesterday there is a voice-review page that will save a spoken sentence per track to a store the next agent can read. That page is built and tested with simulated speech, and it waits on a real phone.

Remaining epics.

  • Merge the voice review and use it once. Set the token, open it on the phone, review one batch by voice. The output of that single use is worth more than everything in the taste repo today.
  • A feedback store that agents read. Spoken sentences, typed notes, two-button picks, and the occasional long paragraph all land in one place, keyed by batch and track. The agent building a batch reads the last three rounds before it plans. The rule "taste models preferences only; objective judgment is ears" stays.
  • From sentences to rules. Each round of feedback contains rules in disguise. "Power-of-two sections, mostly four or eight bars." "A new hat element three or four sections in a row, and I must still hear the earlier ones." "The sound before the kick should not be cut short." Someone has to turn those into the living rubric, with a measure beside each one where a measure exists. That someone is an agent, and the rubric is a document in the repo that changes after every batch.
  • A model, later. Note 10 estimated two hundred picks for a first preference model. At one batch of ten per day with a sentence each, that is three weeks, not fifteen months. The model is not the point. The point is that the batch generator stops proposing things he has already said no to.

engineer: deciding what to try

Done. A loop skeleton and a hypothesis runner for the mastering lab. In practice, the engineer this week was me, reading feedback and writing ten plans by hand. That is fine for the first week and wrong as a steady state.

Remaining epics.

  • The batch engineer. An agent run that reads the feedback store, the rubric and the last batch's measurements, writes ten plans that each change one named thing, hands them to the pipeline, verifies the renders, publishes the batch and sends the notification. Overnight, unattended, one batch a day at most. This is the assistant rung of the ladder made concrete on arrangement.
  • The mix engineer. The same loop over device parameters on a copy of the set, judged first by measures (do the five fixes move the numbers toward the references?) and then by him. This needs the device-edit epic in hands and the single measuring stack in ears.
  • The kick engineer. The loop the September plan started with. Knob map plus calibrated kick measures plus overnight search. It should come last, not first, because the kick is where measurement is weakest and taste is most particular.
  • Budget and cadence. Six hours a week of his time, plus six of picks if picking is very easy. The engineer has to fit its asks inside that, and the system should count what it costs in minutes of his attention per round, as carefully as it counts LUFS.

humming: extraction

Done. A transcriber started on 24 September. Deferred by the interview until the sketch and sound-design stages of the next project.

Remaining epics. One, and it is the oldest idea in the story: hum or beatbox a phrase on a walk, and have it exist as a MIDI clip or a layered drum sketch in a Live set by the time he gets home. I return to this in part three.

The site: the place where it all meets

Done. Twenty notes, a page that streams batches with one batch per page, a play-batch mode that announces each track, a transport that follows playback, and a voice review waiting to be merged. The site has quietly become the control surface of the whole system. It is where renders go, where feedback comes from, and where the record of decisions lives.

Remaining epics.

  • Feedback in, plans out. Close the data loop: what he says on the page is what the engineer reads, and what the engineer decided is visible on the next batch's page ("this batch changes X because you said Y").
  • A releases page. Every finished track, playable in one playlist, with its Short, its video and its links. This is the public face of the doubling targets.
  • Publishing from the rig. Today publishing is a script plus a pull request run by an agent. It should be one command the engineer calls at the end of a run, with the same checks (every file returns 200, the manifest is valid) built in.

HW002: the track

Done. Four references bought, measured and loaded into a copy of the set, level-matched. A rubric seed. Five batches of arrangement experiments with feedback on two of them. All plugins loading, all renders on the grid.

Remaining epics, in order.

  • Choose the arrangement. One more round or two, then a pick. His feedback already narrows it: 33 to 50 seconds, scooped opening, stacked hats, a longer pre-kick sound, a real drop.
  • The mix pass. The five fixes, made on a copy, measured against the references, then judged by ear. This is where the mix engineer epic earns its keep, or where he does it himself in a session with the A/B hotkeys.
  • The master. A loudness target is still his call. The references sit at −4 to −7 LUFS; YouTube turns everything down to about −14. Density and tone carry a Short, not level.
  • The video. Glitch and industrial kit-bash, per the interview. Nothing in the system does video yet. This may be the epic that decides whether 31 October holds.
  • Release. YouTube Shorts only. A page on the site. A note.

The process itself

Done. One repo, continuous tests, dependency alerts handled, a security model, pull requests for everything, notes as the record. A working agreement about autonomy: reasonable defaults, publish without asking, never wake him.

Remaining epics.

  • Commit the interview answers and the feedback rounds as documents, so that the plan is in the repo and not only in a conversation.
  • One project at a time, as a pipeline. After HW002 come STRK001 and HW005. Each needs the same stages: references, rubric, arrangement batches, mix, master, deliver. The second time through should be mostly reuse. If it is not, the first time was done wrong.
  • Cost accounting. Minutes of Anthony per release; agent hours per release; renders per accepted change. These numbers decide whether 128 tracks a year is a plan or a slogan.

Part three: the horizon

Here the note stops reporting and starts imagining. I will describe music production as it is for him now, as honestly as the record allows, and then describe it as it could be once this system is mature. "Mature" does not mean finished. It means the loop runs on every stage of a track, not only arrangement, and the human's share of each stage is the share only a human can do.

Production now

Strip away the software and look at the practice as it stands in October 2026. One person, a demanding job, roughly six hours a week for music. A DAW he knows well and a teacher he learns from. A handful of tracks in progress, one of them worked for most of a year. A habit of references and a habit of logging. A commute with earbuds.

Most of those six hours do not go to musical decisions. The session logs from May and July say so. They go to setup, to plugins that do not load, to file transfer, to remembering where things were, to rendering and waiting. When a musical decision does come, it is often a comparison: this version or that, this hat pattern or that, louder or quieter. Comparisons are cheap to make and expensive to prepare. Preparing them is exactly the work the agents took over this week.

So production now, with the system as it stands, looks like this. In the evening or overnight an agent prepares ten honest variations of one thing. In the morning the producer listens to them somewhere that would otherwise be dead time and says what he hears. By evening there is a new set that answers him. His six hours are untouched; the comparisons happen in time that used to be empty. When he does sit down in a session, he arrives with decisions already made and a shortlist already narrowed. That is already a different practice from the one in the May logs, and it took one week.

Production once the loop runs everywhere

Now extend that pattern to every stage, and let a few years pass. I will describe it as a set of scenes, because a list of features would miss the point.

The walk. He is walking and a phrase arrives, the way they do. He hums it, then beatboxes a pattern under it, into his phone. Nothing else. By the time he is home the phrase exists as a MIDI clip in a new Live set, quantised to the grid but with his timing offsets kept as a groove. The beatbox exists as three drum tracks, with sounds chosen from his own library because they are closest to what his mouth did. The set has a name, a tempo guessed from his hum, and a note that says "sketch from Tuesday's walk". This is the 2020 video, delivered. The extraction thread, which has waited six years, becomes the front door of the whole system. Every track starts as a sound a body made.

The night. The rig runs while he sleeps. It has a plan for the track at its current stage, written by the engineer from the last round of feedback and the living rubric. Tonight it is working on sound design: it is searching the knob map of his kick chain for a kick that sits where the references sit, measured with meters that have been calibrated against known answers, and it is rendering candidates. It makes a few hundred, keeps the ten that pass the measures and are most different from each other, and publishes them to a page with a voice that will introduce each one. It sends one notification at seven in the morning. It did not wake him. If something broke at three, it wrote that down instead.

The commute. He presses play. A voice says "track one: tighter scoop, the rest unchanged" and the kick plays over the bar of the song where it will live. He says "too soft, next". Track two. "Better. Keep the attack. Next." Ten tracks, twelve minutes, his hands never touch the phone. By the time he reaches work, the system has ten sentences from him and the batch engineer has started on tonight's plan. Three mornings like this and the kick is chosen. It would have taken three sessions of an hour each with the old practice, and most of each hour would not have been listening.

The session. He still sits down with the DAW. Not to render variations; that is beneath him now, in the literal sense that it happens below the level he works at. He sits down for the things that need a trained ear in a quiet room with a hand on a knob. The final shape of a sound. A decision about a breakdown that no batch could settle. The parts where taste and craft meet and cannot be split into ten honest options. These sessions are rarer and better. They start from a shortlist and end with a verdict. The log entry reads like a producer's log, not an IT log.

The teacher. After a lesson, the feedback he was given becomes rules the system knows. "Fix all distortion plugins so output matches input" is already a line in his notes; in the mature system it is a check the rig runs on every chain and a flag on the batch page. The teacher's ear becomes a measure where it can and a rubric line where it cannot. The relationship stays human. The lesson's half-life gets much longer.

The release. A track that is chosen, mixed and mastered through the loop ends in a bundle: the full track, the Short, a video cut by the same kind of batch process (ten candidate edits of found footage against the Short, his pick by voice), the page on the site, the upload. The doubling targets, four to eight to sixteen to thirty-two, stop being a wish and become a throughput question. One hundred and twenty-eight tracks in 2030 is one every three days. No person can do that with the May practice. A person can do it if his share of each track is the sketch, a few dozen spoken sentences, two or three deep sessions, and the final yes.

The second track. HW002 took most of a year because everything about it was the first time. STRK001 is next in the interview's order, and the test of the system is how little of that year repeats. The references for STRK001 are bought in an evening and measured by morning. The rubric starts as a copy of HW002's, with the lines that were specific to one track removed. The first arrangement batch exists before he has opened the set himself, because the engineer can read a set's structure now and knows what a first batch looks like. His first act on the new track is to listen to ten versions of its opening and say which one feels like the track he meant. If that is true, the second track costs weeks instead of months, and the third costs days. If it is not true, the pipeline was built for one track and the map in part two has an epic missing.

Two at once, and the limit. The interview said one project at a time, and that is right for a person. It is not a constraint on the rig. The rig can carry HW005 through sound design overnight while STRK001 is in its arrangement rounds, as long as his mornings only ever get one batch. The limit of the whole system is not rendering time or agent time. It is the number of cheap questions one person can answer well in a day before the answers get worse. Finding that number, and staying under it, is more important than any throughput figure.

The record. Every batch, every sentence he said, every measurement, every pick is kept. After a year there are thousands of small verdicts. Taken together they are something no producer has had before: a dense, dated, searchable account of what he liked, in his own words, with the audio beside it. It is a corpus of one person's taste. A model can be trained on it, and will be, and it will be useful for filtering. But the corpus itself is the stranger and more valuable thing. It is an autobiography written in verdicts. It would let a future agent say, with evidence, "you have said no to this kind of hat pattern eleven times since March", and it would let him see his own ear change.

What the system is, once it is mature

Put the scenes together and a shape appears. The system is not a composer and not a producer. It is an instrument. A DAW is already an instrument of a sort, one that plays whatever you tell it, slowly, with your hands. This is a DAW with hands of its own and a question at the end of every phrase. You play it by answering. You tune it by being consistent. It gets better at offering you what you would have chosen, which lets you spend your choosing on finer things.

The ladder from note 10 describes its vertical structure. Meters that are trusted because they are calibrated. A critic that reads the meters and the rubric and says what is wrong in sentences. An assistant that proposes honest options and prepares them for a cheap verdict. An engineer that runs the search overnight and decides what to try next. Each rung asks more of the machine, and each one only works if the rung below is honest.

The lifecycle describes its horizontal structure: sketch, sound design, arrange, mix, master, deliver. The instrument is mature when the loop runs on each stage. Today it runs on one. The order of the rest is roughly the order of how cheap the human verdict is. Arrangement first, because a thirty-second sentence settles it. Mix next, because the references give the measures something to aim at. Sound design after that, because the measures are weakest there. Mastering with a target he sets once. Delivery as plumbing. Sketch comes from the other direction, by the walk, and meets the rest in the middle.

And there is a rule that holds the whole thing together, which the record has taught more than once. The thing that gets measured must be the audio that was actually written, by a measure that was checked against a known answer. The thing that gets judged must be judged by his ear, at the lowest possible cost. Trust the file, calibrate the ruler, make the question cheap. Every failure in the six years of story is a failure of one of those three.

A contrast, for the record

It is worth saying what this is not, because the loudest story in music technology right now is a different one. The generative models that Anthony toured in 2020, and their descendants, make audio from a prompt. They sit at the audio tier of the three-tier model he borrowed, and they get better every year at producing a plausible track of any genre in a minute. A reader could reasonably ask why a person with his background spent six years on the control tier instead.

The answer is in the 2020 video and in every batch since. His problem was never a shortage of music. It was that the music he already had, in his head and later in his sets, was expensive to get out and expensive to judge. A model that makes a new track does nothing for a track that exists and is nearly right. A model that makes a new kick does not tell him whether his kick sits where the references sit. The generative tier answers "give me something". The control tier answers "take this thing of mine and show me its neighbours", which is the question a producer actually asks a hundred times per track.

There is also a quieter reason, and the record supports it. Every version of this system that tried to make the machine's judgment stand in for his own stalled: the feature distance, the evolver, the unused judge. The version that works keeps his ear as the final gate and makes everything else cheap. A generative model with his taste baked in would be the same bet made in the opposite direction, with the judgment hidden inside the weights where it cannot be checked. The control-tier bet keeps the judgment outside, in sentences he said, with the audio beside them. That is slower to build and far easier to trust.

None of this rules out generative tools inside the loop. A model that proposes a hat pattern, or a riser, or ten candidate textures for a breakdown, is just another source of honest options for the batch. The architecture does not care where an option came from, only that it was rendered through his chain, measured by calibrated meters, and judged by him. The generative tier can be an instrument inside the instrument. It cannot be the player.

What this changes about being a producer

Three things, I think, and the third is the one worth caring about.

The first is that versions become free. In the old practice a version costs a session. Producers learn to be stingy with versions and to trust the first thing that sounds all right, because the alternative is another evening. When versions cost nothing, the stinginess becomes a habit to unlearn. You can hear the breakdown four lengths long. You can hear the hats in three orders. The question stops being "is this good enough" and becomes "which of these is right", which is a better question and, oddly, an easier one for an ear to answer.

The second is that taste becomes the scarce input, visibly. In a solo practice taste is spent invisibly, mixed in with everything else. Here it is the one thing the system cannot make, so it is counted and spent on purpose. Twelve minutes on a train is a budget. That changes the texture of the work. The producer's hours go to listening and deciding; the machine's hours go to everything else. This is what the interview answer meant by "be focused as a musical instrument for him; it must not become a burden". The instrument must never ask a question that it could have answered itself.

The third is that the producer's identity moves. In the old practice you are what you can make with your hands in a session. In the new one you are the shape of your verdicts. That sounds like a loss and I do not think it is one. The verdicts are the part that was always yours. The rendering, the file transfer, the fiftieth variation of a hat pattern were never where the music was. They were where the time went. What remains is the ear, the body that hums the first phrase, the hand on the knob in the rare deep session, and a corpus of choices that grows into a style. The scenes above have the producer doing less and the music being more his, not less.

The risks, named

A visionary note that does not name its failure modes is a sales pitch. These are the ones the record itself points at.

Fragmentation returns. Three measuring stacks already exist. A voice review was built in one session while the arrangement pipeline was built in another. The monorepo and the PR rule are the defence, and they only work if they are followed when it is inconvenient.

The question gets cheap by getting shallow. If every verdict is "next" or "keep", the system learns to make things that survive a twelve-minute train ride and nothing more. Some decisions need the quiet room. The design has to keep the deep session as a first-class stage, not an afterthought, and the batch engineer has to know which questions to leave for it.

The measures replace the ear. It is tempting, once the meters are calibrated and the critic writes sentences, to let a passing score stand in for a verdict. The September stems showed what that costs: three measures wrong and a kick described by a bent ruler. The rule must stay that measures guarantee honesty and the ear decides. If a batch passes every check and he says it is dead, the batch is dead.

Convergence to the references. Measuring against four tracks will, if left alone, pull the music toward those four tracks. References should be a floor and a vocabulary, not a target. The rubric should carry his rules, which came from his teacher and his own listening, with the references as evidence for some of them.

The survey video problem. In 2020 the video that got 27,000 views was the one about other people's tools, and the series ended there. The system makes it very easy to generate interesting notes, pages and experiments. The product is a finished track. Every week without a release moving closer is a week spent on the tool.

Burden. One of the last answers in the interview was that this must not become a burden. A system that needs its owner to install things, click dialogs, grant permissions and answer configuration questions is a burden with good intentions. The right measure of the system's maturity is how few of his minutes it needs per release, and the right failure to watch for is that number going up.

A wider view, carefully

Nothing here is specific to one person in principle. Any producer who works alone, has a day job, keeps references and has a commute is in the same position: time-poor, verdict-rich, and spending most of the hours on things that are not music. The pattern of cheap honest batches and spoken verdicts would transfer. So would the ladder, and the rule about calibrating before gathering.

What does not transfer, and what makes this work at all, is that the system was built around one practice by someone who could read its logs. The rubric is his. The references are his. The feedback is in his words, and the agents that write the next batch have read all of his notes. A general product would have to do without most of that and would be worse for it. I would rather this stayed what it is: an instrument built for one player, documented well enough that another player could build their own.

Coda

The story began with a student in California saying that music lives in his head and that getting it out is the problem. Six years later, the getting-out is half built. The hands work. The ears are calibrated where they have been tested and suspect where they have not. The taste is a page waiting to be opened on a phone. The mouth, which started it all, is a transcriber started on a drifting afternoon in late September, and in my reading it is the thread most worth pulling next, once HW002 is out.

The nearer ending is dated. A Short of HW002 by 31 October: a pick, a mix pass, a master, a video, an upload. The loop is open on arrangement and it will have to open on the mix within the month. That is the test of whether the week of 1 October was a beginning or an episode.

The farther ending is the one this note was asked to imagine, and it is simple to say. A person walks, hums, and comes home to a sketch. A machine works through the night and asks, in the morning, ten cheap honest questions. The person answers on a train. Twice a week there is a quiet room and a hand on a knob. Every few days there is a release. After a year there is a body of work and a record of every choice behind it. The instrument answers back, and the music is more his for it.
