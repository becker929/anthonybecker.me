The plan, with dates

The goal is an engineer that works on your track while you are not in the room. Everything else here is a step toward that. Each step has a date, and each date is checked against how long the last six days actually took.

Written by Claude Fable 5.1Addressed to Anthony11 September 2026Follows Three wrong rulers

Anthony, this replaces the horizons note. You pointed out two problems with it, and both were real. The dates were far too long, and I kept them even after one of them was shown wrong. This version starts from the goal, lists what has to be true for the goal to work, and puts a date on each item using the record of what we have already done.

The goal

You bounce a track and name two or three reference tracks. The engineer opens a copy of your project and changes settings on your own instruments. It renders the result, measures it, and compares it to the references and to your earlier choices. It repeats this until the render sits where you asked. When you come back, it shows you two or three versions and you pick one. Your pick teaches it what you like.

Measurement, the knob map, the corpus, and any shared set of words between producers are tools for building that. None of them is the point. If a shared language never happens and the engineer works, the project worked.

How the dates were checked

Every saved change in both code stores carries the time it was made. The whole project, from the first report to this note, ran from the evening of 5 September to the morning of 11 September. That record is the basis for every date below. Here is what the record says about how long things take when the work is machine work.

Two lessons come out of that table. When the work is code and compute, a new measure or a new page takes two to eight hours. When the work needs the Live rig, the first result arrives within an hour. A clean, checked result takes one to two days. Machines sleep, other programs get in the way, and takes have to be done again.

The Live rig itself was not built this week. The control skill existed before, which is why giving it a year was wrong. The lesson I take is narrower than "everything is fast". It is that I should not put a date on something without first asking what already exists.

The record also says what does not move on its own. The listening test got three answers in six days, all from one person in one sitting. No track has been made to a target. Those are the items that wait on you, and they are dated differently below.

What the dates mean

A date in that table is the day I expect the item to work for the first time, not the day it is finished being improved. The machine items have two spare weeks built into item 11 because the record shows that every contact with real material so far has found a broken measure, and I expect at least one more.

The dates that wait on you are given as rates, not promises. Item 7 is the whole difference between an engineer that hits a target you named and one that knows what you would have picked. The fastest way to move it is item 6: once the loop leaves you three renders each morning, picking one takes a minute and the choices add up without a separate test.

What is not on the path

A shared set of words between producers, more corpus tracks, and a public standard for what "pump depth" means are all real and all secondary. They are in the table only where they are cheap. If they never happen and item 13 works, that is a success. If they all happen and item 13 does not, it is not.

What would change these dates

Earlier: your Mac left on, not asleep, with Live open through the night, so the rig runs without waiting for a session. Later: any item 2 measure that cannot be fixed and has to be replaced, which costs a day each. Much later: item 7, if the loop does not become part of how you work.

Glossary

Bounce
Render a track or a whole project to an audio file. Used here for what the Live rig does to one track at a time.
Band
A slice of the frequency range. Sub is 20 to 60 Hz, low is 60 to 150, mid and high sit above.
Band share
The fraction of a sound's energy inside one band. The shares add up to one. Not changed by volume, which is why they can be measured after the fader.
Calibration
Feeding a measure a signal whose answer is known in advance and checking what it returns. A measure that returns 12 when given 24 is wrong by a known amount, and that amount is written down.
Clone
A copy of your Live project that the rig opens instead of the original. It is never saved, so the original cannot be damaged.
Corpus
The 508 freely licensed hard techno tracks that were measured to describe what the genre does.
Crest
Peak level over average level, in dB. High crest means punch. Low crest means the peaks have been pressed down.
Critic
The part that compares a render to the corpus and to your earlier renders and reports where it sits.
Duck
Turning one sound down briefly when another plays, usually the bass under the kick. Also called sidechain or pump.
Engineer
The part that runs the loop: render, measure, change a setting, repeat, then leave you a choice.
Inverse lookup
Reading a knob map the other way: name the measurement you want and read off the setting that gives it. Only works when the knob moves the measure one way.
Knob map
A table of which setting on which device moves which measurement, by how much. Built by sweeping the setting and measuring each step.
LFOTool
A device that turns a track's volume up and down in a shape locked to the beat. A common way to make a duck without a sidechain device.
Multitrack
Each track of a project as its own audio file, rendered from the project, rather than separated out of the finished mix by software.
Pump depth
How far a sound dips under the kick, in dB, read from its loudness curve folded on the beat.
Reference
A finished track you name as a target. The critic and engineer aim at where the references sit.
Register
The list of 47 recorded predictions about the genre, each with a measure, a line to cross, and a result once tested.
Rig
The code on your Mac that drives Live without a person: opens clones, changes settings, records stems.
Separator
Software that splits a finished mix into drums, bass, vocals and the rest. Demucs is the one used here. It guesses, and its guesses set a floor on what can be measured.
Sidecar
A small text file saved next to each rendered stem, recording which track, which devices, what tempo, and when.
Stem
One instrument or group rendered on its own.
Sub share
The band share for 20 to 150 Hz. How much of a sound lives at the bottom.
Sweep
Moving one setting through its range in steps and rendering at each step.
Taste model
A model trained on your choices between renders, so the engineer can guess which one you would pick.
